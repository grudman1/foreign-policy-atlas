How this atlas is built

What this is

An interactive, president-by-president assessment of U.S. foreign policy. The central question is causal: did this president improve or worsen America's strategic position with respect to each country, relative to the trajectory already in motion when they took office?

The map reads on two independent axes: color is where the relationship stands now; the arrow is what this president caused, measured against the trajectory they inherited. This is not a warmth scoreboard, and not advocacy in either direction.

Magnitude (modest / material / major) applies only to helped / hurt and shows in the arrow's weight on the map. The two axes are independent — the combination is the point: a country can be adversarial-but-helped (an enemy handled well), or core-ally-but-hurt (a friend damaged).

The counterfactual

Every effect is measured against one fixed counterfactual: the continuation of the inherited trajectory, absent the president's major policy changes. That trajectory is recorded in each entry's inherited field. We don't shop for a flattering or damning counterfactual — the inherited path is the only baseline.

Outcome vs. effect

Outcome describes what happened to the U.S. position. Effect describes what the president caused. A good outcome a president merely inherited is not "helped" — the effect is "mixed" with role Inheritor. A bad outcome the president could not have changed is not "hurt." Credit and blame are applied symmetrically — to wins, losses, allies, and adversaries.

Honest blanks

Not every country needs a scored effect. Setting effect: "unscored" with a reason is more credible than manufacturing a judgment. The four reasons:

  • no material effect — nothing of strategic consequence happened
  • not driven by the president — things changed, but not because of the president
  • systemic forces only — driven by global or structural forces
  • insufficient evidence — we cannot source a defensible call

An honest blank beats a guess. Triage-fairness rule: if we score the comparable wins, we score the comparable losses, and vice versa. Neglect can itself be the critique — a president who ignored a country where engagement mattered is "hurt" (role Neglect), not "unscored."

How each entry is made

Every entry runs through a sourced, multi-model pipeline designed to guard against the LLM failure mode: a well-written, plausible, unsourced claim. Claims are fetched, not recalled.

  1. Researcher — builds a dated, web-sourced evidence pack from primary reporting.
  2. Analyst — drafts the entry from that pack only. No recall from training data.
  3. Red-team — a model in a different family adversarially attacks the call (state, effect, magnitude, attribution).
  4. Editor — reconciles analyst and red-team reads.
  5. Calibrator — audits consistency: does the same standard apply to wins and losses, allies and adversaries?

When analyst and red-team disagree on a marquee call, a human adjudicates.

Honesty built in

  • Every scored entry carries its strongest counterargument — the case for the opposite scoring.
  • Contested calls are flagged ("reasonable analysts could score this differently").
  • No partisan framing; loaded adjectives in either direction get cut.
  • Where a placement is set at the editor's direction against the stricter analytic read, the entry says so.
  • Where the sign of an effect depends on which grand-strategy school you hold (primacy / liberal-internationalist / restraint), the entry discloses that in grandStrategyDispute rather than silently picking one.

Scoring tools

The five levers — a checklist, not a weighted formula; scored only where relevant:

  1. Security / threat — deterrence, force posture, war/peace
  2. Leverage / dependence — who needs whom; coercive leverage gained or lost
  3. Rival-denial — denied China / Russia / Iran a foothold, or handed them one
  4. Coalition / institutional — alliances, blocs, institutions strengthened or eroded
  5. Economic / tech — trade, capital, critical minerals, technology standards

Role taxonomy (how the president related to the outcome): Architect, Accelerator, Closer, Stabilizer / Active Stabilizer, Inheritor, Bystander, Neglect, Spoiler.

Known limitations

  • Omission bias. Errors of commission (things done) are easier to see than errors of omission (things neglected). The fixed counterfactual helps but does not eliminate this.
  • Triage-selection bias. What we choose to score, and what we leave unscored, is itself a judgment.
  • Grand-strategy tilt. Some effects only have a clear sign once you assume a school of grand strategy.
  • Projections, not predictions. Best / base / downside outcome lines are explicitly projections — the spread is the point.
