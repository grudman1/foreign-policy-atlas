01 — ORIENTATION

What the Atlas measures

For every country, the map answers two questions at once — and keeps them strictly apart. The first is about the world as it stands. The second is about the person in the Oval Office.

The central research question is causal: did this president improve or worsen America's strategic position with respect to this country, relative to the trajectory already in motion when they took office? That framing does most of the work in this document. It is the difference between grading a result and grading a decision — between what happened on someone's watch and what they actually caused.

The color · "state"

Where does the relationship stand now?

A point-in-time snapshot of the current relationship — ally, rival, or somewhere between — independent of who caused it.

The arrow · "effect"

What did this president do about it?

The president's net causal effect on the U.S. position over the term, measured against the path they inherited.

A relationship can stand in a bad place that no recent president caused, or a good place that one of them quietly squandered. Holding the two axes apart is what lets the Atlas describe both honestly — and it is the reason a single map cell can say two independent things without the encodings fighting for the same channel.

02 — THE TWO AXES

Color and arrow

State — the color

The state is a snapshot of where a relationship stands today, regardless of how it got there. Six values cover the spectrum from treaty alliance to open hostility.

core-allyTreaty-level or institutionally deep.
alignedA partner, moving together on most issues.
neutralTransactional; no strong pull either way.
strainedCooling, damaged, or low-engagement.
adversarialActive rivalry or hostility.
usThe United States itself.

Effect — the arrow

The effect is the president's net causal effect on the U.S. strategic position over the term, judged against the inherited trajectory. The public map uses only four values — no five-level scale. False precision is worse than honest coarseness.

↑ helpedImproved the U.S. position versus the inherited path.
— mixedNet effect is genuinely mixed, offsetting, or unclear.
↓ hurtWorsened the U.S. position versus the inherited path.
· unscoredDeliberately off the scoreboard — see Triage.

Magnitude — the weight

Magnitude is a separate field — modest material major — shown on hover and carried in the weight of the arrow: a major effect draws a double chevron, material a single bold one, modest a lighter stroke. It applies only to helped and hurt. On the map, mixed is a level bar and unscored draws no mark at all.

ValueMeaning
modestNoticeable but limited in scope.
materialMeaningful — affects the core bilateral dynamic.
majorA fundamental shift in the relationship.
Why the axes are independent

The two axes are independent, and the combination is the whole point. A country can be adversarial but helped — a rival a president handled well — or a core-ally but hurt — a friend whose trust, leverage, or coordination they eroded. Collapsing the two would hide exactly the cases worth seeing.

↑ adversarial · helped

A rival who stayed a rival — but whose position relative to the U.S. weakened on this president's decisions.

↓ core-ally · hurt

Still an ally on paper, but a president eroded the trust, leverage, or coordination that made the alliance valuable.

Color is state; the arrow is effect. The arrows are deliberately monochrome — white with a dark outline. Hue is reserved entirely for state; the arrow carries effect through direction and shape alone.

03 — THE CAUSAL RULE

Cause, not credit

One fixed counterfactual

Every effect is measured against a single, fixed counterfactual: the continuation of the inherited trajectory, absent the president's major policy changes. That baseline is recorded in each entry's inherited field. The rule is deliberately strict — there is no shopping for a flattering or a damning counterfactual. The inherited path is the only baseline against which a president is graded.

Outcome is not effect

The Atlas separates two things that are easy to conflate:

FieldCaptures
outcomeWhat happened to the U.S. position — the world, regardless of cause.
effect + pointsWhat the president caused — the attribution, argued from sourced facts.
Credit and blame are symmetric

A good outcome a president merely inherited is not helped — the effect is mixed and the role is Inheritor. A bad outcome they could not have changed is not hurt. Unearned gains and blameless losses both resolve toward mixed. The same standard applies to wins and losses alike.

In practice · China, fentanyl

The U.S. overdose decline is a real, favorable outcome — but the Atlas does not bank it as a presidential win. The reporting traces the supply shift to enforcement that began in 2023, before the term, so the improvement is attributed to inherited momentum, not 2025 tariffs. The same logic runs in reverse on Taiwan: record cross-strait military pressure tracks a tempo that predates the term, so it is largely not counted against the president either. Outcomes move; only the share a president caused moves the arrow.

04 — TRIAGE

The right to stay silent

Not every country needs a scored arrow. Rather than manufacture a judgment, an entry can be marked unscored with an explicit reason. This is a feature, not an evasion — a blank is more credible than a fabricated verdict.

unscoredReasonUse when
noMaterialEffectNothing of strategic consequence happened.
noPresidentialEffectThings changed — but not because of the president.
systemicOnlyMovement was driven by global or structural forces.
insufficientEvidenceNo defensible call can be sourced.
The triage-fairness rule

If you score the comparable wins, you must score the comparable losses — and vice versa. The unscored pile is a selection-bias surface, and it gets audited. Crucially, neglect can itself be the critique: a president who ignored a country where engagement mattered may be hurt with role Neglect, not unscored. There is no "not a priority" escape hatch.

05 — SCORING TOOLS

The five levers

There is no formula and no weighted sum. Two instruments structure the judgment: a checklist of five levers to test where a president actually moved the position, and a vocabulary of roles to name how they related to the outcome.

Security & threat

Deterrence, force posture, war and peace. key: security

Leverage & dependence

Who needs whom; coercive leverage gained or lost. key: leverage

Rival-denial

Did this deny China, Russia, or Iran a foothold — or hand them one? key: rivalDenial

Coalition & institutional

Alliances, blocs, and institutions strengthened or eroded. key: coalition

Economic & tech

Trade, capital, critical minerals, technology standards. key: economicTech

In practice · a sign-disputed lever

Levers don't always point one way. Pressuring NATO allies to spend more is a single coalition move with two defensible signs: it erodes allied trust and predictability (a coalition loss) and it can force long-deferred commitments that leave the alliance stronger (a coalition gain). When a lever's sign is genuinely contested like this, the entry records both readings rather than silently picking one — and the cell is flagged contested.

06 — THE VOCABULARY

The nine roles

The role field names the president's relationship to the result — the difference between building an outcome and simply receiving one. Effect says which direction; role says how they got there. There are exactly nine, and the engine may never invent a tenth.

Architect built it

Constructed a genuinely new outcome — structure that did not exist on the inherited path.

Example · Saudi Arabia — helped, materialA Major Non-NATO Ally designation, a signed Strategic Defense Agreement, and a state-partnership accession are scored as newly built architecture, not the continuation of a prior trend.
Accelerator sped a trend

Pushed an already-moving trajectory faster or harder, without originating it.

Example · China — mixedFast, high tariffs accelerated an already-steepening decoupling that began under the prior administration; the engine credits the acceleration, not the invention of the trend.
Closer finished a deal

Brought an inherited initiative across the line — signed, ratified, or operationalized what a predecessor had negotiated.

IllustrativeA president who signs and brings into force an agreement substantially negotiated before the term.
Stabilizer held the line

Maintained an inherited position through pressure without new construction — steadiness as the contribution.

IllustrativeA relationship kept on its existing footing through a destabilizing period, with no new initiative claimed.
Active Stabilizer worked to hold it

Spent real effort and capital to keep a relationship from slipping — stabilization as a deliberate act, not mere inertia.

Example · Poland — helped, modestSustained force presence, new financing, and head-of-state engagement actively held a frontline relationship in place against countervailing pressure.
Inheritor received it

Took the benefit (or cost) of a result they did not cause. The default landing place for unearned gains.

Example · Egypt — mixedGaza co-mediation, the financing anchor, and recurring military exercises were all inherited; continuation is not a presidential effect.
Bystander uninvolved

Present but not a mover — the relationship's path ran independent of anything the president did.

IllustrativeA country whose trajectory simply did not respond to U.S. presidential action during the term.
Neglect harmful inaction

Inaction that itself did damage — the cost of not engaging where engagement mattered. Pairs with hurt, never with silence.

NoteThis is why "not a priority" is not an escape hatch: consequential neglect is scored as harm, not left blank.
Spoiler broke something

Actively damaged a working relationship or arrangement.

Example · Belgium — hurt, modestNew tariff barriers on a treaty ally's leading export sector are scored as damage to the economic relationship, against an otherwise stable alliance.

Examples drawn from the live Trump 2nd-term dataset are illustrative of how the role is applied; see the colophon on the dataset's scenario framing. Roles labelled "Illustrative" describe the definition generically.

08 — THE PRODUCTION ENGINE

How an entry is made

No entry is written in one pass. Each country moves through a multi-stage pipeline in which a single artifact is progressively hardened — source pack → draft → critique → reconciliation → calibration — and the stages are split across separate model contexts on purpose, so that no single author both forms a judgment and signs off on it.

Three non-negotiables

The Analyst gets a clean context — the pack only, no priors and no research commentary. The Red-team is a genuinely different model family, not another prompt to the same one — the engine refuses to run if it is pointed at a Claude or a lightweight model. And every claim carries exactly one source; an abstention beats a guess.

R
Researcher Claude Opus 4.7 Claude Sonnet 4.6 ×8

A lead researcher coordinates eight bounded sub-agents — one for the inherited baseline and one for each of seven policy angles (diplomacy, security, economic statecraft, technology & export controls, human rights, regional dynamics, and early-vs-late time cuts). Each runs a fixed loop on Sonnet 4.6: propose targeted primary-source searches, retrieve via a primary-source-first engine, write a sourced memo, then take exactly one reflection pass to close the single most important gap. The lead model, Opus 4.7, synthesizes the memos into one dated evidence pack — baseline, what happened, attribution signals, contested points, and an explicit gaps section. It renders no score. It gathers and cites facts, and builds fresh rather than confirming prior framing.

A
Analyst Claude Opus 4.7

Working only from the pack — no outside knowledge — the Analyst produces the draft entry: outcome separated from effect, one source per bullet, the strongest honest counterargument against its own call, and unscored preferred to a guess. Its output passes through a deterministic validation gate that enforces the schema mechanically — valid enums, magnitude present only for helped/hurt, one bracketed source per point. A failing draft is returned to the Analyst with the specific violations, up to three times.

↻ bounded self-correction loop · Analyst ⇄ validation gate · max 3 attempts
×
Red-team OpenAI GPT-5.4

An adversarial reviewer on a different model family attacks the draft — the sign, the counterfactual, any unsourced claim, double-counting across levers, and triage-fairness — and ends by naming the single strongest argument the Analyst must answer. Decorrelation is the point: a critic trained by a different lab is the cheapest correction for a model's own training-era blind spots. The engine hard-refuses to run this stage on a Claude or a downgraded model.

E
Editor Claude Opus 4.7

The Editor reconciles draft and critique against the pack. For each objection, the draft either survives because the pack defends it, or the call moves toward the more conservative reading — and a genuinely unresolved dispute becomes contested: true, with the disagreement preserved in the counterargument. A standing rule guards against over-correction: a flagged-but-true claim is re-sourced, never silently deleted. Where the critique exposes a gap a fresh adversarial pass should test, the entry loops back to the Red-team.

↻ bounded editor ⇄ red-team loop · later rounds attack the reconciled entry · max 3 rounds
⟂
Adjudication Claude Sonnet 4.6

When the Analyst and Red-team land on different effect signs, the disagreement is resolved by a dedicated adjudication pass that applies the counterfactual test directly — reading the sourced points as primary evidence and deciding whether the relationship is materially better, worse, or genuinely ambiguous versus the inherited baseline. Its rationale is stored with the entry, and if it overrides the prior reading it must supply a fresh counterargument against its own call.

C
Calibrator Claude Opus 4.7

Across the finished set, a calibration pass checks cross-country consistency — is magnitude used alike, are comparable situations scored alike, is the unscored pile balanced rather than quietly hiding one direction of result? It reports flags and suggested revisions for review; it never silently rewrites a verdict. This is the layer that keeps a country-by-country method honest as a whole map, not just one cell at a time.

Every stage writes a durable audit artifact — pack, draft, critique, reconciliation, adjudication rationale, final — so any verdict can be traced back through the exact reasoning that produced it.

09 — ANALYTIC STANCE

A balanced national interest

The Atlas aims for a balanced national-interest assessment and refuses to adopt a single school of grand strategy as its house view.

  • It does not assume the goal is always a closer or warmer relationship. Sometimes distance, pressure, or restraint serves U.S. interests.
  • It does not treat alliances as automatically good or automatically a "subsidy." The liberal-order case (alliances as strategic assets) and the realist/restraint case (interests over values; avoid overextension) are both legitimate lenses.
  • Where the sign of an effect depends on which school you hold — primacy, liberal-internationalism, or restraint — the entry says so in grandStrategyDispute rather than quietly picking one.
The sign of a policy's effect sometimes depends on the strategy you bring to it. When it does, we disclose the dispute — we don't resolve it by fiat.
In practice · a grand-strategy dispute

Take a withdrawal from a peripheral commitment. A primacy reading scores it ↓ hurt — ground ceded to a rival. A restraint reading scores the same move ↑ helped — overextension avoided, power conserved. Neither is "wrong"; the disagreement is about ends, not facts. The Atlas surfaces that in grandStrategyDispute rather than laundering one school's preference into an objective-looking arrow.

10 — CREDIBILITY

Source & honesty rules

These are the rules the whole project rests on. The characteristic failure of a language-model author is not shallowness — it is fluent confabulation: a well-written, plausible, entirely unsourced claim. Everything here is built to catch that.

Source rules

Claims are fetched, not recalledEvery points bullet traces to reporting captured in sources. Concrete, datable facts over vibes.
Be honest about sufficiencyThe evidence field is set truthfully. If sourcing is insufficient, the entry is unscored.
Prefer unscored to a guessA blank is more credible than a fabricated judgment.
Red-team the cellsEntries get a second, adversarial read from a different model family than wrote them.

Honesty rules

Always show the downsideEvery scored entry carries a real counterargument. None usually means it is mis-scored.
Mark contested callscontested: true wherever a placement rests on judgment, or where serious analysts would differ.
Don't dress judgment as factA disputed placement must not imply certainty the evidence won't support.
No partisan framingDescribe what happened and its effect on U.S. interests — no loaded adjectives, no cheerleading.
11 — BEYOND THE CELL

Regions, projections, systems

A country arrow is the core unit, but three layers sit around it — and one rule governs how they relate.

LayerWhat it holds
RegionsA narrative layer per region — its tilt, dynamics, flagship projects, the U.S. goal, and the stakes stated as both the upside and the damage case.
ProjectionsA best / base / downside spread for a relationship's future — explicitly projections, not predictions. The spread is the point; a single forecast would overclaim.
Global systemsA reserved panel for effects that don't live in any one country — alliance architecture, financial power, the trade order, tech standards, nonproliferation, maritime, energy and minerals.
In practice · one policy, many cells

Some effects don't live in a single country. A pact like AUKUS helps Australia (deeper capability and integration), hurts France (the contract rupture it triggered), and shifts the Indo-Pacific balance against China — one decision, three cells with different signs. The Atlas links the affected country cells with linkedPolicies and a crossTheaterTradeoff note so a single trade-off isn't mistaken for unrelated country events.

The map is not a scoreboard

Bilateral cells are not independent and they do not sum to a grade. One policy can produce several country effects and a systemic one; counting greens against reds would double-count trade-offs and reward whoever drew the most cells. The systems layer and the cross-policy links exist precisely so a reader doesn't reduce a strategy to arithmetic.

12 — LIMITATIONS

What this can't fix

The method reduces error; it does not abolish it. Four limitations are disclosed rather than pretended away — because a map that names its own blind spots is more trustworthy than one that claims to have none.

LimitationWhy it persists
Omission biasThings done are easier to see than things neglected. The fixed counterfactual helps but does not eliminate this.
Triage-selection biasWhat we choose to score, and what we leave blank, is itself a judgment. The triage-fairness rule is a guardrail, not a guarantee.
Grand-strategy tiltSome effects only have a clear sign once you assume a school of strategy. Disclosure surfaces this; it cannot make it neutral.
Projections, not predictionsThe best / base / downside lines are deliberate spreads of plausibility. They will be wrong in detail; the range is the honest part.
13 — END TO END

A worked example: China

To see the whole engine in one pass, follow a single cell from empty to final.

  • Researcher. Eight sub-agents assemble a dated, sourced pack: the inherited "compete" framework and ~21% baseline tariffs; the escalate-then-truce cycle; two summits and a time-bound truce; the rare-earth dependence; the legal challenge to the tariff authority. No verdict is rendered — only facts and an explicit list of what could not be established.
  • Analyst. Working from the pack alone, it separates the outcome (a rivalry left roughly where it stood) from the effect (what the tariffs and summits actually caused), and drafts a mixed call with role Accelerator — the trend was sped, not invented.
  • Red-team (a different lab's model). Attacks the sign: the cleanest attributions — coalition strain, exposed dependence, the legal invalidation — point toward hurt, while the apparent wins are contaminated or reversible.
  • Editor. Reconciles against the pack. The downside survives as the dominant counterargument, but the import-reduction reading is real enough that the net stays genuinely offsetting; the call holds at mixed and the cell is flagged contested: true.
  • Adjudication & Calibration. The counterfactual test confirms no decisive net change in trajectory; the calibration pass checks that comparable rivalries are held to the same bar. magnitude stays null — correct for a mixed call.

The result is the cell shown in Section 07: adversarial · mixed · Accelerator · contested, with a sourced causal argument, an explicit downside, and a recorded confidence — every step of which can be audited back to the pack.

Colophon

A living contract

The Foreign Policy Atlas is an interactive map rating U.S. foreign policy president by president. This document describes Version 3.0 of its methodology — the two-axis President-Effect model — as of May 2026. Color encodes the current state of a relationship; the arrow encodes one president's causal effect on the U.S. position, measured against the trajectory they inherited. Where the evidence cannot support a verdict, the Atlas leaves the arrow off and says why. The model is versioned alongside the data and revised as the assessment matures.

The Trump 2nd-term dataset is an analytic projected-timeline scenario: entries are sourced to that scenario, and outcome lines are projections, not predictions. The methodology — the axes, the causal rule, the levers and roles, the multi-model engine, and the honesty rules — applies identically to any president and any timeline.
